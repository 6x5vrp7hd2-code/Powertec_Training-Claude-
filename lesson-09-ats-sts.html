ATS / STS and Alternate Supplies

Read this in full before attempting the scenarios below — they assume this material.

The mechanism, not just the concept, of resilient paths

Resilient paths only work in practice because of the switching device that actually executes a transfer between sources. An ATS uses electromechanical contactors and typically interrupts supply briefly during transfer — on the order of 50 to 100 milliseconds. An STS uses solid-state switching and transfers within a fraction of a cycle, fast enough to be effectively seamless to IT load, which is why STS is the standard choice for dual-corded critical loads while ATS remains common on generator-side and less transfer-sensitive circuits.

Make-before-break versus break-before-make

A make-before-break transfer briefly connects both sources to the load in parallel — a genuine paralleling event, made safe only because the switch has already confirmed both sources are synchronised in voltage, frequency and phase before allowing the overlap. A break-before-make transfer avoids paralleling entirely but introduces a short interruption instead. Knowing which mode a given unit uses changes what 'isolated' and 'safe to test' actually mean during that transfer.

Modes that defeat the whole point of the device

An ATS or STS left in manual or inhibited mode after maintenance will not transfer automatically on a real fault, no matter how healthy the standby source is. This is one of the most consequential and most avoidable failures in the entire programme: the device works perfectly, the standby source is healthy, and the load still drops, because nobody re-armed automatic mode before closing out the permit.

Diagnosing a failed transfer without assuming a fault

When a transfer switch doesn't transfer during a test, the instinct is to assume the switch has failed. The more reliable first step is to check whether it's simply in manual or inhibited mode, whether both sources are actually presenting healthy voltage to it, and whether an interlock or permissive signal is blocking the transfer — because these explanations are both more common and easier to fix than an internal fault.
