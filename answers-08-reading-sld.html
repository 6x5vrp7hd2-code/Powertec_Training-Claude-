Answers · Section 08

Reading a Single Line Diagram

Model answers and multiple-choice key.

Multiple Choice — Answer Key

1. What does the perpendicular bar symbol at a switching device’s fixed contact represent, per IEC 60617?
Correct answer: B — An isolating function with a guaranteed visible break

2. What combination of symbols indicates a circuit breaker “suitable for isolation”?
Correct answer: C — Both the cross (X) AND the perpendicular bar

3. Why must the legend of a site’s single line diagram always be confirmed before a manoeuvre, according to the guide?
Correct answer: B — Different sites mix IEC, ANSI and in-house conventions, so the same drawing can mean a switch in one place and a disconnector in another

4. What does the tag “QF2-RECT” indicate, following the described tagging convention?
Correct answer: D — Circuit breaker number 2, serving the rectifier input function

5. What do chevrons on either side of a breaker symbol indicate?
Correct answer: A — A withdrawable (draw- out) device

Long Answer — Model Points

1. Explain the “additive” logic of IEC 60617 switching device symbols: how do the bar, circle, cross, and chevron marks combine to describe a device’s full capability?

Each mark encodes one specific capability: the perpendicular bar means a guaranteed, visible off-load break (isolating function); the small circle means on-load breaking/making capability (a switch); the cross (X) means short-circuit breaking capability (a true circuit breaker); chevrons mean the device is withdrawable. A device’s full symbol combines whichever of these marks apply — e.g., a switch-disconnector shows both the bar and the circle, since it can isolate AND switch rated current, while a plain disconnector shows only the bar, since it cannot break any current at all.

2. Why does the guide insist that “doubt about a symbol is cleared BEFORE the manoeuvre, never during”? What could happen if this rule is ignored?

If a specialist proceeds with an unclear or misread symbol, they might attempt to isolate load with a device that has no breaking capacity (a disconnector), causing an arc flash and explosion, or might believe a device provides a guaranteed isolating break when it does not, leaving a supposedly “isolated” circuit still connected. Confirming the legend beforehand — with the operator, if unfamiliar with the site — removes this ambiguity before any physical action is taken, when a wrong guess has no consequence yet.

3. Describe the meaning and purpose of a device tag such as “QF2-RECT,” and explain why using the exact tag matters more than a description like “the breaker on the left.”

“QF2-RECT” precisely and unambiguously identifies breaker number 2 serving the rectifier-input function, tied to both the diagram and the physical label on-site. A vague description like “the breaker on the left” depends entirely on the observer’s orientation and current layout, and is exactly the kind of ambiguity that historically leads to “I thought it was the other feeder” accidents (referenced later in section 18.1) — using the exact tag removes any dependence on memory, position, or assumption.

4. Compare IEC and ANSI single line diagram conventions in general terms, and explain the practical risk of a specialist assuming one convention when the site actually uses the other.

IEC conventions typically use a cross for a breaker and specific bar/circle/chevron combinations for isolating and switching functions; ANSI (American) conventions often use a rectangle or half-circle for a breaker and different overall diagram layout conventions. Many sites mix both traditions with their own in-house additions. A specialist assuming one convention on a site actually using the other could misidentify what a device is capable of — for example, treating a rated-current-only switch symbol as if it were a full short-circuit-breaking breaker — with potentially serious consequences if that device is then operated on load or under fault conditions.

5. Why is knowing how to read a single line diagram described as “an absolute prerequisite” for understanding interlocks and MOPs?

Interlocks and MOPs are written entirely in terms of the diagram’s tags, symbols and the sequence of device positions they represent — a MOP instruction like “open QF12, confirm K1 released” only makes sense to someone who can read what QF12 is, what “open” looks like on the symbol, and why K1 is linked to it. Without fluency in reading the SLD, a specialist cannot verify that a written procedure matches reality, cannot safely improvise if something doesn’t match, and cannot independently confirm the physical state of equipment against its intended design.
