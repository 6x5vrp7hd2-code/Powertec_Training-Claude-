Section 20

Before / During / After — The Operator’s Checklists

The three-phase checklist structure (before, during, after) mirrors a discipline used across virtually every high-reliability operational domain — aviation pre-flight/in-flight/post-flight checks, surgical time-out procedures, and industrial process safety “pre-job briefings” all follow the identical logical structure the guide applies here to electrical operations.

Additional context:

Pre-job briefings (toolbox talks) as a documented safety practice. The “team briefing (toolbox talk)” the guide lists as a before-operation requirement is a globally recognised safety practice across construction, industrial, and utility work — a short, structured meeting immediately before a task begins, covering roles, sequence, specific risks, hold points, and the stop criterion, ensuring every participant shares the same mental model of the operation before the first action is taken, rather than relying on each person having separately read and correctly interpreted the written MOP beforehand.

The “counted removal” principle for locks and earths. Requiring that the number of locks and earths removed at the end of an operation exactly match the number applied at the start is a direct, simple, and highly effective error-detection mechanism against the single most common LOTO-related failure mode: forgetting to remove (or, more dangerously, forgetting that one was never actually applied in the first place, leaving equipment believed-isolated actually live) a lock or earth. This principle is universal across LOTO/isolation frameworks worldwide precisely because a simple count mismatch is an unambiguous, easily verified signal that something has gone wrong.

“Announce aloud, confirm” verbal protocol. The guide’s requirement that each action be announced aloud and confirmed by a second participant before proceeding (“I am

opening QF12” / “confirmed, QF12”) mirrors verbal challenge-and-response protocols used in aviation cockpits and nuclear plant control rooms, specifically because verbalising an intended action forces a moment of conscious deliberation (reducing “autopilot” slips) and gives a second person an explicit opportunity to catch an error before it becomes irreversible.
