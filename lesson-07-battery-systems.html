Lesson · Module 07

Battery Systems

Read this in full before attempting the scenarios below — they assume this material.

A battery is a source, not a load — say it plainly

Every other piece of equipment in this programme can, in principle, be isolated from its supply. A battery cannot be isolated from itself. Disconnecting it from the DC bus removes its connection to the rest of the circuit, but the chemical reaction generating voltage inside the cells continues regardless. This single distinction is the reason battery work is treated differently from every other isolation in this programme.

Stored energy that isolation procedures don't remove

A disconnected string still presents a genuine arc-flash and shock hazard at its own terminals, because the stored energy hasn't gone anywhere — it's simply no longer connected to the DC bus. Any procedure that treats 'disconnected' as equivalent to 'safe' is missing this distinction. Safe battery work depends on manufacturer-specific handling procedures, not on the standard isolate-prove-earth sequence used elsewhere.

Chemistry changes the hazard profile

VRLA and lithium-ion batteries fail differently, and treating them identically is itself a hazard. Lithium-ion carries a materially higher thermal runaway and fire risk, with suppression and reignition considerations that don't apply in the same way to VRLA. Knowing which chemistry is installed on a given string — and what that specifically changes about the response to an alarm or a fault — is a precondition for working safely around it.

Reconnection is a checked event, not a formality

Bringing a battery string back onto a live DC bus after maintenance requires confirming correct polarity, string integrity, and a state of charge close enough to the rest of the bus to avoid a large circulating current. A string reconnected without these checks isn't just a paperwork gap — a reversed or damaged string closed onto a live bus is a genuine fault condition, not a completed maintenance task.
