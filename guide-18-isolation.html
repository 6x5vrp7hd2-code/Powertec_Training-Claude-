Section 18

Isolation — From the Order of Operations to the Hand-Over

The five-step isolation sequence (separation, locking/tagging, identification, voltage absence test, earthing) is the direct European/French equivalent of what North American practice calls “Lock-Out Tag-Out” (LOTO) under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147, and the specific sequencing logic — never a coincidence of naming — reflects the same underlying safety engineering across every jurisdiction referenced in section 15.

Additional context:

OSHA’s LOTO standard (29 CFR 1910.147) and its structural parallel to the guide’s five steps. OSHA’s control-of-hazardous-energy standard requires, in essence: identification of all energy sources, notification of affected employees, shutdown, isolation, application of lock/tag, dissipation/verification of stored energy (capacitors, springs, hydraulic pressure, gravity — directly paralleling the guide’s section 6 on stored energy), and a verification step confirming the equipment is actually de-energised before work begins — mapping almost one-to-one onto the guide’s five-step sequence, despite arising from an entirely separate regulatory tradition (US OSHA vs. French NF C 18-510/EN 50110).

“Zero energy state” as the universal underlying goal. Regardless of which national framework’s terminology is used (French “consignation,” EN 50110’s isolation procedures, or OSHA’s LOTO), the underlying engineering goal is always the same: reaching and verifiably proving a “zero energy state” across every possible source — electrical, but also (in the wider LOTO tradition) mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal and gravitational stored energy. The guide’s insistence that a UPS retains

dangerous stored energy in its capacitors and batteries even after AC isolation (section 6, revisited in 11.3 and 18.6) is a direct electrical-domain instance of this universal “don’t forget the other energy sources” principle.

Two-stage isolation and role separation. The guide’s distinction between a “first stage” (separation and locking, performed by the isolation officer) and steps carried out later by the work leader (identification, voltage absence test, earthing) reflects a deliberate safety-engineering choice to separate the person who isolates the equipment from the person about to work on it — a second, independent set of eyes verifies the isolation before work begins, rather than trusting a single person’s assessment throughout the entire process.
