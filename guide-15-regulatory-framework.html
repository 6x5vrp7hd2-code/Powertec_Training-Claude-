Section 15

The Regulatory Framework and Electrical Qualifications

The regulatory landscape described here — a patchwork of national and regional frameworks all converging on the same underlying principle (formal, named, verifiable competence before any electrical work) — reflects genuinely global regulatory convergence even where the specific mechanisms differ sharply.

Additional context, current as of the most recent editions of the referenced standards:

NFPA 70E’s 2024 edition and OSHA’s 2024 update. NFPA 70E (electrical safety in the workplace) and a related OSHA rule update both took effect in 2024, representing the first major arc-flash-related regulatory refresh in over two decades. Key changes include a strengthened requirement for proactive, documented risk assessments and PPE-selection rationale for every energised work task above 50 V, an explicit clarification that a closed panel door is generally NOT sufficient on its own to eliminate PPE requirements during switching or abnormal-condition operation (directly relevant to the guide’s racking and switching guidance in sections 9.4 and 17), and a requirement that anyone within the arc flash boundary wear hearing protection regardless of whether they are actively performing the work themselves.

NFPA 70E’s two PPE-selection methods. The standard offers two independent, non- mixable methods for a given piece of equipment: the Incident Energy Analysis method (calculating the specific cal/cm² a worker could be exposed to at a defined working distance, then selecting PPE with an arc rating at least equal to that figure) and the Table (Category) method (using pre-defined equipment/task/voltage combinations to look up a required PPE category directly, valid only when the equipment’s actual voltage, fault current and clearing time fall within the table’s specified ranges). An employer must choose and consistently apply one method per piece of equipment, and document the process — mixing methods for the same equipment is explicitly non-compliant.

IEC 61936 and EN 50110 as the European counterpart to NFPA 70E/OSHA. While North America centres on NFPA 70E and OSHA regulations, the equivalent European framework is built on EN 50110-1 (operation of electrical installations — defining roles, isolation procedures, and competency requirements at a pan-European level) combined with national implementations such as France’s NF C 18-510 (described in detail in section 15.2) and Germany’s DGUV Vorschrift 3/VDE 0105-100. All of these frameworks share the same fundamental logic even though the specific qualification symbols and administrative mechanisms differ substantially from country to country — which is precisely why the guide’s section 15.5 “country overview” is necessary for anyone operating internationally.
