Section 21

The Red List — The Mistakes Never to Make

The fourteen items in the guide’s “red list” function as a condensed summary of the entire guide’s safety logic — each one is a direct violation of a principle established in an earlier section, collected together as an unambiguous, memorable final checklist. This kind of “never do this” list is a recognised technique in safety training generally: a positive procedure (how to isolate correctly) is necessary but not always sufficient to prevent error under pressure or unfamiliarity, whereas a short, absolute “never” list is easier to recall in the moment something starts to go wrong.

Additional context connecting each red-list item back to its root cause elsewhere in the guide:

Items 1–3 (operating a disconnector on load, closing an earth without a test for dead, racking a closed breaker) all stem directly from confusing what a switching device can and cannot physically do (section 9.1) — the single historical root cause the guide identifies for serious accidents.

Items 4–5 (defeating an interlock, relying on a mimic/BMS/VPIS instead of a physical test) both violate the core interlock and voltage-absence-test principles established in

sections 17 and 18.3 — trusting an indication rather than a proof.

Items 6–7 (forgetting a source, paralleling non-synchronised sources) directly reflect the stored-energy (section 6), UPS multi-source (section 11.3), and synchronisation physics (section 13.1) material.

Items 11 and 13 (launching an operation with redundancy already consumed; creating two simultaneous neutral-earth links) are the direct practical consequences of skipping the residual load calculation (section 12) and the neutral-transfer discipline (section 5.8) respectively.

Item 14 (opening a UPS cover before discharge time and DC test for dead) is the direct consequence of misunderstanding the capacitor/battery stored-energy hazard from section 6 and 18.6.

In industrial incident-investigation terms, nearly every item on this list corresponds to a well-documented, recurring root cause found in real electrical accident databases internationally (OSHA incident reports, HSE RIDDOR data in the UK, and similar bodies elsewhere) — none of these are hypothetical risks invented for training purposes; each reflects a pattern of real historical accidents.
