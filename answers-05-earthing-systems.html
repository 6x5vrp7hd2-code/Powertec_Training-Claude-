Earthing Systems — What Happens to the Fault Current?

Model answers and multiple-choice key.

Multiple Choice — Answer Key

1. In the IEC 60364 two-letter code, what does the first letter describe?
Correct answer: B — How the transformer/source neutral is connected to earth

2. Why is TN-S considered the standard for critical data centre distribution?
Correct answer: C — It produces reliable, high-magnitude fault currents that trip an ordinary breaker quickly, without needing an RCD

3. What is the defining risk of the TN-C system when used downstream of a UPS?
Correct answer: B — Neutral current flows through bonded frames and shields in parallel with the PEN conductor, risking noise, heating and a dangerous PEN rupture scenario

4. What happens at the first insulation fault in a well-designed IT earthing system?
Correct answer: D — Almost no current flows; the installation keeps running while an IMD raises an alarm

5. What is “PME” (Protective Multiple Earthing), as commonly used in UK/Irish public LV distribution?
Correct answer: A — A TN-C-S arrangement: combined PEN conductor upstream, separated into N and PE at a multiple-earthed link near the customer

Long Answer — Model Points

1. Explain, in sequence, what happens to the fault current in each of the four earthing systems (TT, TN-S, TN-C, IT) at the moment of a first insulation fault.

TT: current returns via soil through two separate earth electrodes, is small (a few to tens of amps), requires an RCD to detect. TN-S: current returns via the low-impedance copper PE, behaves like a short-circuit (thousands of amps), tripped quickly by an ordinary breaker. TN-C: same fast trip as TN-S, but because the PE and neutral are the same PEN conductor, part of the ongoing neutral current also flows through bonded frames in parallel — a persistent hazard beyond just the fault event. IT: almost no fault current flows (only a small capacitive leakage), nothing trips, and an IMD raises an alarm while the installation continues running.

2. Why must an installation never be converted from TN-S back to TN-C (“only in the direction C to S”)? Explain the physical reasoning.

Going back from TN-S to TN-C downstream would mean re-combining a separate PE conductor (which by design carries zero current in normal operation and is a clean, safe reference for equipment) with a neutral conductor that is already carrying real load and harmonic current. This would immediately introduce dangerous neutral current into all bonded frames downstream — precisely the hazard TN-S was designed to avoid — so the direction of conversion is a one-way, non-reversible safety rule.

3. Describe why a data centre typically contains several different earthing systems simultaneously, giving at least three examples of where different systems might be used within the same site.

A data centre is a stack of independently sourced sub-networks, each created at a point where a transformer, generator, UPS inverter, or isolation transformer establishes a new neutral point: e.g., the utility’s MV network (often impedance-earthed, outside data centre control), each MV/LV transformer secondary (typically configured TN-S), each generator (its own neutral point, relevant during transfers), and specific critical auxiliary circuits (e.g. safety/control systems) that may be run in IT for continuity of service.

4. Explain the “neutral during source transfers” problem: why does a 3-pole (unswitched neutral) transfer between two earthed sources create circulating currents and nuisance ground-fault tripping in a TN-S system, and how does a 4-pole transfer solve this?

In a 3-pole transfer, both source neutrals stay permanently connected to the switchboard neutral, so when both are earthed, the ongoing neutral current has two parallel return paths: through the neutral conductor and through the earth/PE path between the two source earths. This “circulating current” through the PE makes the ground-fault (G) protection — which expects a zero vector sum of phase and neutral currents on a healthy circuit — see what looks like an earth fault, tripping needlessly. A 4-pole transfer switches the neutral along with the phases, so only one source’s neutral- earth link is ever connected at a time, eliminating the second return path and the resulting fictitious ground-fault signal.

5. Compare the relative safety and practicality trade-offs of TT, TN-S, TN-C, and IT for a critical data centre load, referencing at least one advantage and one drawback of each.

TT: simple and safe for people via RCD, but critical IT loads’ cumulative leakage currents cause nuisance tripping, making it unsuitable downstream of UPS/PDU. TN-S: fast, reliable tripping without RCD dependency and a clean (zero-current) PE reference — the data centre standard — but requires an extra conductor and strict discipline on maintaining a single neutral-earth point. TN-C: saves copper upstream but is forbidden downstream of critical equipment because of neutral current in bonded metalwork and the PEN-rupture risk. IT: preserves continuity of service through a first fault (valuable for targeted critical auxiliaries), but needs a dedicated fault-finding organisation, IMDs, and is limited in network extent by capacitive leakage growing with cable length.
