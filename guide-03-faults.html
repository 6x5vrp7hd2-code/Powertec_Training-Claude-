Section 03

Faults — Overload, Short-Circuit, Earth Fault

The three-fault taxonomy (overload, short-circuit, earth fault) maps directly onto the “LSIG” protection functions detailed later in section 9.5 (Long time = overload, Short time/Instantaneous = short-circuit, Ground = earth fault) — this is not a coincidence: circuit breaker trip units are literally designed around these three fault families because each has a distinct current magnitude, timescale, and required response.

Additional context:

Arc flash physics. The guide’s description of arc flash (electrodynamic forces, arc temperatures of “several thousand degrees,” pressure waves) is well grounded: arc temperatures can exceed 19,000°C — roughly four times the surface temperature of the sun — vaporising copper and creating a supersonic pressure wave. This is why arc flash incident energy calculations (covered in section 16) are treated as a distinct, serious hazard category separate from simple electric shock.

Prospective short-circuit current (Isc) and standards. The short-circuit study referenced is required practice under IEC 60909 (short-circuit current calculation methodology) internationally, and under IEEE 141/242 (the “Red Book”/“Buff Book”) in North American practice. The output — the prospective Isc at each point in the installation — is what determines the minimum breaking capacity (Icu, developed in section 9.2) that switchgear at that point must have.

Overload vs short-circuit timescales. The guide’s figures (overload at 1.1–3× rated current, tripping over seconds to minutes; short-circuit reaching tens of thousands of amps in milliseconds) reflect the fundamentally different thermal time constants involved: cable insulation can tolerate a moderate overcurrent for a limited time before dangerous heating occurs, whereas a bolted fault current bypasses any thermal buffering — the risk is instantaneous mechanical and thermal destruction, not gradual ageing.
