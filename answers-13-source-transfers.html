Answers · Section 13

Source Transfers and Couplings

Model answers and multiple-choice key.

Multiple Choice — Answer Key

1. What is the approximate formula for the current that flows when paralleling two out-of- synchronisation sources?
Correct answer: B — I = ΔU ÷ X (voltage difference divided by link impedance)

2. Why is paralleling two feeders from the SAME busbar generally safe without a synchro- check relay?
Correct answer: B — They carry, at every instant, essentially the same voltage (ΔU ≈ 0), since they share a common origin

3. What does the synchro-check relay (ANSI 25) verify before permitting a breaker to close and parallel two sources?
Correct answer: C — Matching voltage, frequency, and phase angle (and confirmed phase sequence) within a defined window

4. What is “reverse power” protection (ANSI 32) designed to detect?
Correct answer: C — A generator’s prime mover losing power while still electrically connected, causing the generator to be driven as a motor by the other source

5. What is the fundamental danger of paralleling two non-synchronised sources?
Correct answer: A — It is equivalent to creating a short-circuit between the two sources, with destructive currents proportional to their voltage difference

Long Answer — Model Points

1. Explain, using the ΔU ÷ X relationship, why a 180° phase difference between two sources being paralleled is equivalent to a short-circuit at twice the network voltage.

The current flowing between two paralleled sources is proportional to their instantaneous voltage difference (ΔU) divided by the link impedance (X). At a 180° phase difference, the two sinusoidal voltages are at their maximum opposition at every instant, making the effective voltage difference twice the peak voltage of a single source — electrically indistinguishable from connecting a source directly across a short-circuit at double its own nominal voltage, producing enormous, destructive currents.

2. Describe the four synchronisation conditions the guide lists, and explain which one is structural (cannot be corrected in the field) versus which are dynamically monitored during the paralleling operation.

Same phase sequence (rotation) is structural: it is fixed by how the source is wired internally and verified at construction or after any cabling change — it cannot be corrected “in the moment” during a paralleling operation, only prevented by careful commissioning discipline. Same voltage, same frequency, and near-zero phase angle are all dynamic conditions that vary continuously and must be actively monitored (via AVR settings, governor control, and the synchro-check relay/auto-synchroniser) at the actual instant of closing.

3. Explain the difference between closed transition (make-before-break) and open transition (break-before-make) transfers, and describe the specific additional risk that closed transition introduces which open transition avoids.

Closed transition briefly parallels the two sources (typically for no more than ~100 ms) under synchro-check control before fully transferring, avoiding any interruption to the load — but this introduces the paralleling risk itself: if synchronisation is not genuinely present or the parallel duration extends beyond the studied limit, the brief parallel period becomes a real hazard. Open transition instead fully opens the first source before closing the second, guaranteeing the two sources are never connected together at all, avoiding the paralleling risk entirely — at the cost of a guaranteed (if brief) break in supply to the load.

4. Why does a “studied prolonged parallel” with the public utility grid require a grid operator agreement, reverse power protection, and validated combined short-circuit current, according to the guide?

A prolonged parallel with the public grid means the site’s generator(s) and the utility are electrically connected for an extended period, which requires validating that the combined short-circuit contribution of both sources does not exceed the installed switchgear’s breaking capacity (Icu), that a protection plan including reverse power (protecting the generator’s prime mover) and decoupling (protecting the utility’s network integrity and personnel safety during any utility-side fault) is in place, and that the neutral-earthing arrangement across both sources is correctly resolved — none of which a site can unilaterally decide, since it directly affects the utility’s own network and legal/contractual obligations.

5. Using the real case of an STS closed-transition bypass, explain why the MOP must always have the operator verify synchronisation before authorising the manoeuvre, even though interlocks and a synchro-check relay are already in place.

Even with interlocks and a synchro-check relay physically preventing an out-of- synchronisation closure, the MOP still requires the operator to independently verify synchronisation (via measurement or “sources in sync” indicator lamps) because relying solely on automated protection removes the human cross-check that catches a genuinely failed or miscalibrated relay, a wiring error, or a situation the automated logic wasn’t designed to detect — the guide’s broader philosophy (echoed in the red list, section 21) is that no single indicator, however automated, is treated as sufficient proof on its own for a high-consequence action.
