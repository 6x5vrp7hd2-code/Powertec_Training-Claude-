Answers · Section 09

Switching Devices and Their Performance

Model answers and multiple-choice key.

Multiple Choice — Answer Key

1. What can a disconnector (isolator) NOT do?
Correct answer: D — Break a current, even a small one — operating it on load causes an arc and explosion

2. What does Icu (ultimate breaking capacity) represent?
Correct answer: A — The maximum short-circuit current a breaker can interrupt without destroying itself, verified by an O-CO test, with no guarantee of continued service afterward

3. Why must HRC fuses always be replaced in sets of three, even if only one has blown?
Correct answer: A — The other two fuses suffered the same thermal stress and may be weakened even though they did not melt

4. What is the single most dangerous mistake associated with draw-out circuit breakers?
Correct answer: C — Racking a closed (loaded) breaker in or out

5. What is the defining feature of a switch-disconnector (load break switch)?
Correct answer: B — It can break and make rated current, but it must withstand a short-circuit closed rather than break it

Long Answer — Model Points

1. Explain the difference between a disconnector, a switch-disconnector, and a circuit breaker in terms of what each device can safely interrupt, and why confusing them is described as a historical cause of serious accidents.

A disconnector provides only an off-load, visible isolating break — it has zero current- interrupting capability, so operating it on load produces an arc and can explode the cubicle. A switch-disconnector adds the ability to break and make rated current safely (like a heavy-duty light switch), but it must withstand a short-circuit passing through it closed, not break one. A circuit breaker can interrupt everything, including short-circuit current, up to its rated breaking capacity (Icu). Confusing these — most dangerously, treating a disconnector as if it could interrupt load current — has historically caused severe arc flash accidents, because the device simply lacks the physical mechanism (arc-quenching chambers, adequate contact separation speed) to safely break current under load.

2. Describe the difference between Icu and Ics, and explain why a coupling operation between two half-busbars requires a study of combined short-circuit currents against these values.

Icu is the maximum fault current a breaker can interrupt at all, verified once via an O-CO sequence, with no requirement that the device remain serviceable afterward. Ics is the fault current the breaker can interrupt while remaining fit for continued service, verified via the more demanding O-CO-CO sequence, expressed as a percentage of Icu. When two half-busbars are coupled, the combined short-circuit contribution from both sources adds together, potentially pushing the prospective fault current at the coupling point above the Icu (and certainly above the Ics) of installed devices — a study is required to confirm the coupled configuration’s fault current stays within what the switchgear can actually handle.

3. Explain the purpose of the striker pin on an MV switch-fuse combination, and why it matters that it trips all three phases rather than just the phase where the fuse blew.

The striker pin is a mechanical component released when an HRC fuse melts; it physically trips the associated switch on all three phases simultaneously. This matters because if only the blown phase were disconnected, the transformer would be left running “single-phased” — receiving power on only two of its three phases — which causes severe overheating and unbalanced currents in the transformer windings and any connected three-phase motors; tripping all three phases together avoids this entirely.

4. Describe the three positions of a draw-out circuit breaker (connected, test, disconnected) and explain which one is required for isolation and why.

Connected (racked in): both main and auxiliary circuits are engaged — normal operation. Test: the main circuit is separated but auxiliary circuits (controls, releases) remain connected, allowing functional testing without energising downstream equipment. Disconnected (racked out): both main and auxiliary circuits are separated, providing a visible, lockable break — this is the position required for isolation, since it is the only one offering a guaranteed, verifiable break in both power and control circuits.

5. Explain the LSIG protection functions found in a modern electronic trip unit, using one example setting for each function.

L (long time): thermal-image overload protection — e.g., Ir = 0.9×In with a 16 s trip delay at 6×Ir, tolerating brief overloads like battery recharge current. S (short time): delayed short-circuit protection for selectivity — e.g., Isd = 6×Ir with a 0.3 s delay, allowing a downstream breaker to clear first. I (instantaneous): no-delay protection for faults on the switchboard itself — e.g., Ii = 12×In, tripping immediately since no downstream device could be involved. G (ground fault): detects a residual current imbalance suggesting a downstream earth fault — e.g., Ig = 0.2×In with a 0.4 s delay, though this can be triggered fictitiously during neutral-coupling operations (section 5.9).
