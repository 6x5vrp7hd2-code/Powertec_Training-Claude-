Section 13

Source Transfers and Couplings

The physics of paralleling two AC sources — and the catastrophic consequence of doing so without synchronisation — is one of the oldest and most rigorously codified areas of power systems engineering, governed internationally by IEEE C37.90/IEEE C50.12 (generator

protection and synchronising practices) and, in the UK, by the Engineering Recommendation G59/G99 for embedded generation connecting to distribution networks.

Additional context:

The synchro-check relay (ANSI 25). This function — referenced throughout the guide as the device that verifies voltage, frequency, phase-angle and phase-sequence match before permitting closure — is one of the oldest standardised protection functions in the ANSI/IEEE device numbering scheme (IEEE C37.2), reflecting how long paralleling hazards have been formally recognised in the power industry; the fundamental physics (I = ΔU ÷ X, with a 180° phase difference producing an effective short-circuit at twice nominal voltage) has not changed since early 20th-century AC power system design.

Closed vs. open transition and utility interconnection rules. Utilities worldwide typically require any customer-side closed-transition (make-before-break) transfer involving momentary parallel with the public grid to be explicitly agreed and protected against reverse power flow, anti-islanding, and fault contribution concerns — which is why the guide notes that “studied prolonged parallel… with the public network” requires “a grid operator agreement” as a hard precondition, not merely good practice. Closed- transition transfers are popular specifically because they avoid even the brief break- before-make interruption of open transition, which — while often just tens of milliseconds — can still be enough to disturb sensitive UPS or IT equipment upstream of any local UPS protection.

Reverse power protection (ANSI 32). When a generator is running in parallel with the utility or another generator, if its prime mover (engine/turbine) loses power while the electrical connection remains closed, the generator can be driven as a motor by the other source instead of generating — this “motoring” condition can damage the prime mover (particularly turbines) and is specifically what reverse-power protection (ANSI function 32) is designed to detect and trip on, which is why the guide lists it among the required protections for any studied, prolonged generator/utility parallel operation.
