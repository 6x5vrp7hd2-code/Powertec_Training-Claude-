Section 16

Personal Protective Equipment and Instruments

The PPE and instrument categories described (insulating gloves, arc-rated clothing, voltage detectors, measurement categories) are governed by a dense but internationally consistent set of standards, and recent (2024) updates to the arc-flash side of this framework are directly relevant to how the guide’s tables should be applied today.

Additional context:

NFPA 70E 2024 arc-flash PPE category structure. The four PPE categories, defined by minimum arc rating in cal/cm², are broadly: Category 1 (minimum ~4 cal/cm², e.g. arc- rated shirt and pants for lower-hazard panel operations), Category 2 (minimum ~8 cal/cm², arc-rated coveralls plus face shield for tasks like MCC work), Category 3 (minimum ~25 cal/cm², a full arc flash suit and hood, e.g. for switchgear racking), and Category 4 (minimum ~40 cal/cm², maximum protection with a heavy arc suit). Crucially, the arc rating of the selected PPE must always be equal to or greater than the calculated (or table-derived) incident energy for the specific task and equipment — never simply “whatever category matches the voltage class,” since incident energy depends heavily on fault current and clearing time, not voltage alone.

The arc flash boundary concept. This is the calculated approach distance from a potential arc source at which incident energy reaches 1.2 cal/cm² — a widely used threshold associated with the onset of a second-degree burn on exposed skin. It is explicitly not a “safe line” in the way a shock-protection boundary might suggest; it is a threshold at which PPE and other controls become mandatory for anyone crossing inside it while equipment is energised and work is being performed, and simply standing further back during a switching operation genuinely reduces risk (incident energy falls

off sharply with distance).

Measurement category (CAT) ratings and their real purpose. The CAT II/III/IV classification the guide references relates specifically to the transient overvoltage an instrument is designed to withstand at different points in a power system — not simply “how tough” a meter is generally. CAT II covers receptacle-level/plug-connected equipment; CAT III covers fixed distribution equipment such as panels and MSBs; CAT IV covers the origin of the installation (utility service entrance, metering, outdoor conductors) where the highest transient energy from the utility side can appear. Using a lower-CAT-rated instrument at a higher-CAT point (e.g., a CAT II multimeter inside an MSB) risks the instrument failing violently — exploding, effectively — if a transient surge occurs during the measurement, which is precisely the guide’s warning.
