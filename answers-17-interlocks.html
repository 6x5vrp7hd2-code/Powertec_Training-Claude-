Interlocks — Read a Diagram and Never Be Caught Out by Them

Model answers and multiple-choice key.

Multiple Choice — Answer Key

1. What is the fundamental principle of a trapped-key interlock system?
Correct answer: A — A key can only be removed from a lock if the associated device is in a required, safe position — possessing that key is physical proof of that safe state

2. In the “2 keys for 3 breakers” busbar tie interlock, why can the two incomers never be paralleled through the tie?
Correct answer: C — Because only two keys circulate among three locks, so closing the tie always requires releasing a key from one of the two incomers first, meaning both incomers can never be closed simultaneously with the tie also closed

3. What does “VPIS off = indication only” mean in relation to an interlock?
Correct answer: C — The VPIS lamps going off unlocks certain interlocks, but this is an additional safety layer, not proof of a dead circuit — the mandatory voltage absence test is still required

4. What four things must be documented whenever an interlock is deliberately defeated (neutralised)?
Correct answer: A — Written authorisation of the operations manager, a dedicated risk analysis, compensating measures, and a traced restoration at the end of the operation

5. What is Zone Selective Interlocking (ZSI)?
Correct answer: A — A wired logic system in which a downstream breaker that detects a fault signals the upstream breaker to wait, since the downstream device is already handling it

Long Answer — Model Points

1. Explain why a trapped-key interlock is described as providing “physical proof,” and contrast this with a purely procedural or administrative safety rule that relies on someone remembering a step.

A trapped key can only be physically removed from its lock once the associated device has actually reached the required safe position — this is a mechanical fact, not a claim someone makes or a step on a checklist that could be skipped or forgotten. A purely procedural rule (“always check the breaker is open before closing the earth”) depends entirely on human diligence every single time; a trapped-key system makes the wrong sequence physically impossible to perform in the first place, removing the human-error pathway entirely.

2. Using the earthing switch example, describe step by step how the trapped-key mechanism makes it physically impossible to close an earthing switch onto a live circuit, and impossible to re-energise a circuit while its earth is closed.

Key K1 is only released from the upstream breaker’s lock once that breaker is confirmed open — while the key is out, the breaker itself is mechanically locked open (cannot be reclosed). That same key K1 is the only key that can unlock the earthing switch, so the earth can only be closed once the breaker is verifiably open. Once the earth is closed, K1 becomes trapped in the earthing switch’s lock, meaning the breaker (which requires K1 to unlock its own reclosing mechanism) cannot be reclosed while the earth remains closed — making both “closing the earth on a live circuit” and “re-energising with the earth still closed” physically impossible, not merely against procedure.

3. Explain the three families of interlock (mechanical trapped key, electromechanical, logic/PLC) and give one data centre example of each.

Mechanical trapped key (Castell, Ronis, Kirk): a physical key only releases once a device reaches a required position, e.g. the UPS MBB key only releasing once the load is confirmed on static bypass. Electromechanical (solenoid, locking coil): an electric lock only releases given an electrical permissive signal, e.g. a racking lock that only releases once the VPIS confirms no voltage is detected. Logic (PLC, control relays): a programmed condition prevents an action entirely in software/relay logic, e.g. the prohibition of closing utility and generator breakers simultaneously outside the verified synchronisation window.

4. Describe why “an interlock that resists is an interlock talking to you,” and explain the correct response when a manoeuvre unexpectedly resists during a MOP.

An interlock is specifically designed to make a manoeuvre impossible when a required safety condition is not met — so if a manoeuvre unexpectedly resists, the overwhelmingly likely explanation is that some genuine safety condition is not actually satisfied, not that the interlock itself is malfunctioning or “in the way.” The correct response is to stop and diagnose which specific condition is missing (rather than force, bypass, or “lend” a key to get past it), since forcing past a resisting interlock removes exactly the safety barrier the interlock exists to provide.

5. Explain the purpose of the key register and key exchange diagram, and describe what an “active familiarisation” walkdown involves when arriving at an unfamiliar site with dozens of keys.

The key register is a controlled document listing every key’s tag, the specific safety condition it proves, its normal home location, and its current state — kept updated at every manoeuvre so an empty hook or missing key is immediately noticeable and investigated. The key exchange diagram shows precisely which key releases which lock and in what sequence — the authoritative document defining the imposed order of operations. An “active familiarisation” walkdown on arrival at an unfamiliar site involves physically photographing every cubicle front, lock, and label; walking through a complete operational sequence on paper while pointing at the exchange diagram without actually operating anything; and observing real key exchanges during routine periodic tests (generator tests, transfers) to build genuine, verified familiarity before ever writing or executing a MOP on that equipment.
