Answers · Section 12

Calculating the Residual Load — Method and Worked Examples

Model answers and multiple-choice key.

Multiple Choice — Answer Key

1. What is the four-step method for calculating residual load, in order?
Correct answer: A — Measure the real load at the worst expected moment; count surviving equipment; project the redistributed load; compare against the site threshold

2. In Example B (N+1 already consumed), why is the operation forbidden?
Correct answer: C — With UPS 4 already faulted, only two modules would survive isolating UPS 2, pushing each to 103% of rated capacity — a certain overload

3. Why does the guide specifically warn against calculating residual load using a “quiet Tuesday” or night-trough measurement?
Correct answer: C — Because the real risk moment is the worst plausible combination of factors (peak IT load, battery recharge, cooling peak), not an average or low-demand period

4. In a 2N topology, what must be verified BEFORE isolating one complete path, in addition to the load percentage calculation?
Correct answer: B — That the transfer actually works: STS in auto mode, servers genuinely dual-corded across both paths, no orphan single-corded load on the path being isolated

5. Why does kW vs kVA (power factor) matter as a “trap” in residual load calculations?
Correct answer: A — UPS and cable ratings are fundamentally current/kVA-based, so working only in kW can understate the true thermal stress on surviving equipment

Long Answer — Model Points

1. Walk through Example A (N+1 nominal case) step by step, showing how the 69% figure is derived and why it is compared against an 85% threshold.

Total measured load is 620 kW across 4 UPS of 300 kW each (55% capacity nominally, or 155 kW/module = 52% before isolation). Isolating UPS 2 leaves 3 modules to carry the unchanged 620 kW total: 620 ÷ 3 ≈ 207 kW per module, which is 207 ÷ 300 = 69% of each surviving module’s rated capacity. Since 69% is below the site’s 85% policy threshold, the operation is authorised, with the calculated margin explicitly documented in the MOP as the justification.

2. Explain why Example B (N+1 already consumed) is forbidden, and describe what would need to happen (repair or waiver) before the operation could proceed.

UPS 4 is already faulted (on static bypass) before the proposed isolation of UPS 2, meaning only 2 modules (UPS 1 and 3) would actually be left carrying load if UPS 2 were also isolated. Splitting the full 620 kW across just 2 modules of 300 kW each gives 310 kW per module — 103% of rated capacity, a certain overload. The operation must be forbidden until either UPS 4 is repaired (restoring three healthy survivors) or a formal waiver is obtained with an accompanying load reduction to bring the projection back under threshold.

3. In Example C (2N), explain why simply calculating “80% ≤ 90% threshold, therefore authorised” is not sufficient on its own, and what additional verification is required.

The 80% figure only accounts for total power capacity; it says nothing about whether the load will actually and completely transfer to path B in practice. If the STS is not genuinely set to auto mode, if some servers are miswired as single-corded rather than dual-corded across both paths, or if an “orphan” single-corded load exists only on path A, isolating path A could leave real equipment without power even though the aggregate capacity math looks fine — hence the guide’s insistence on verifying the actual physical/logical transfer readiness, not just the percentage.

4. Describe the specific risk illustrated in Example D (distributed redundancy) when the load transfer scheme is non-uniform (e.g., 60% of an isolated block’s load routes to one specific surviving block rather than splitting evenly).

If the cross-transfer scheme routes load unevenly (e.g., 60% of block C’s load specifically to block A rather than splitting evenly between A and B), a specialist who assumes uniform sharing could seriously underestimate the load landing on the specific block that receives the disproportionate share. In the guide’s example, this pushes block A to 85% — borderline against an assumed safe uniform-sharing calculation of only 80% — demonstrating why the current, accurate load transfer scheme (not an assumption of even splitting) must always be consulted for distributed redundant topologies.

5. List and explain at least four of the “traps of load calculation” the guide warns about, and explain why each could cause a specialist to underestimate real risk.

(a) kW vs kVA/power factor: working only in kW can understate true current-based stress, since cables/UPS are kVA/current-rated. (b) Load measured in the night trough while work occurs at peak: night-time measurements dramatically understate daytime peak demand, giving false confidence. (c) Battery recharge current after a generator test or outage: this adds substantial extra load for hours afterward, on top of the normal IT load, and is easy to overlook if only “IT load” is measured. (d) Planned customer deployment growth during the maintenance window: capacity that looks fine today may already be committed to new equipment going live during or shortly after the work, silently eroding the assumed margin.
