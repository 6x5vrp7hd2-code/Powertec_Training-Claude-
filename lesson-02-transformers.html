Lesson · Module 02

Transformers

Read this in full before attempting the scenarios below — they assume this material.

The conversion point, and why direction matters

A transformer's HV side and LV side behave very differently when you're isolating for work. HV-trained engineers instinctively think of energy flowing one way — from the network into the site. In a data centre, a transformer's LV side can be a genuine second source of energisation, because LV boards are frequently paralleled, bus-tied, or backed by generation. Isolating a transformer means considering both directions, not just the direction power normally flows.

Backfeed through the windings

A transformer's windings don't stop conducting just because the HV breaker is open. If the LV side remains connected to a live bus — through a bus-tie, a parallel transformer, or a generator connection — voltage can appear back at the HV terminals through the winding's transformation ratio, even with the HV switch fully open. This is precisely why HV isolation of a transformer is never sufficient on its own in a resilient LV design; the LV side has to be isolated, proven dead, and earthed as a separate step.

Redundancy arrangements: 1-for-1 versus N+1

Some sites pair each transformer directly with its own LV board; others run transformers in an N+1 arrangement where several transformers share a common bus with spare capacity built in. The arrangement changes what 'isolating a transformer' actually removes from service, and it changes what backfeed paths exist. Knowing which arrangement applies to a given transformer on this site is a precondition for planning its isolation safely, not a detail to work out afterwards.

Proving dead on both sides, and what commissioning checks confirm

A transformer isolation isn't complete until both the HV and LV sides have been independently proven dead and earthed. Separately, when a transformer is newly commissioned or being paralleled with an existing unit, the checks that matter — phase rotation, vector group, voltage and phase-angle synchronisation, and protection coordination — exist because getting any one of them wrong risks closing two out-of-step sources together, which is a fault condition, not a successful energisation.
