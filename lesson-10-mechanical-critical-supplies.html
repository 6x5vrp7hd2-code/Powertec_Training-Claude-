Mechanical Critical Supplies (AHU / CRAC)

Read this in full before attempting the scenarios below — they assume this material.

Two separate systems sharing one piece of equipment

An AHU or CRAC unit is simultaneously an electrical load and a mechanically and BMS-controlled device. Electrically isolating it removes its power supply; it does nothing to the control and interlock signals that govern whether the unit is permitted to run. Mechanically shutting it down does the reverse. Treating these as one combined isolation, rather than two separate ones that both need proving, is where this module's risk concentrates.

Why a unit can appear dead but still move

If a fan, pump or damper continues to move after electrical isolation, the most likely explanations are that the wrong breaker was isolated, or that a mechanical drive connection exists that the electrical isolation was never going to touch. Residual momentum should decay quickly; continued movement is a signal to stop and re-verify the isolation, not a detail to note and continue past.

Defining the handover point, precisely

Combined electrical and mechanical work depends on both permits agreeing, explicitly, on exactly where electrical responsibility ends and mechanical responsibility begins — typically at the local isolator and proving-dead point for electrical, with everything downstream (the fan, damper, pump internals and drive mechanism) under the mechanical permit. A handover point that exists only informally, rather than being written down and cross-referenced between the two permits, is a gap waiting to be found the hard way.

Reinstating control state, not just power

A unit re-energised after work isn't safely back in service until it's also correctly reinstated into the BMS control loop. A unit that's electrically live but sitting outside its normal control sequence can behave unpredictably — running continuously, failing to start when needed, or simply not reflecting its true status to anyone monitoring the site remotely.
