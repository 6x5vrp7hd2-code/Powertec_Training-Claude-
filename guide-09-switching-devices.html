Section 09

Switching Devices and Their Performance

The distinction between disconnector, switch-disconnector, circuit breaker, HRC fuse and earthing switch is codified internationally: IEC 60947 (low-voltage switchgear and controlgear) and IEC 62271 (high-voltage switchgear) both define these device categories by their precise interrupting and isolating capabilities, which is exactly the “what it can do / what it cannot do” table the guide provides.

Additional context:

Breaking capacity standards (Icu, Ics, Icw, Icm). These four values are all formally defined in IEC 60947-2 for low-voltage circuit breakers. Icu (ultimate breaking capacity) is verified with an O-CO test sequence and does not guarantee the device remains serviceable afterward — it is a “survival,” not a “comfort,” rating, exactly as the guide states. Ics (service breaking capacity), expressed as a percentage of Icu (commonly 25/50/75/100%), is verified with the more demanding O-CO-CO sequence and does guarantee continued service, which is why specifying equipment with Ics = 100% of Icu (rather than a lower percentage) is often a deliberate design choice for critical data centre distribution, ensuring the breaker remains usable after clearing its rated maximum fault.

HRC fuse classes and international naming. The gG/aM/gR-aR classification the guide describes is the IEC 60269 fuse-link classification system: “g” fuses provide general- purpose protection against both overload and short-circuit, while “a” (partial-range) fuses protect only against short-circuit and must always be paired with another overload-protective device (commonly used for motor and transformer back-up protection). The “gR/aR” ultra-fast semiconductor-protection classes are specifically designed for the very fast let-through energy limits needed to protect power-electronics devices like UPS rectifiers, inverters and static transfer switches — components that can be destroyed by fault energy an ordinary fuse would let through before clearing.

Draw-out breaker positions and arc flash statistics. Racking operations (moving a breaker between connected, test, and disconnected positions) are consistently identified in industry incident data as one of the higher-risk switching operations for arc flash events, precisely because the breaker’s main and auxiliary circuits are being mechanically engaged or disengaged while — if the “never rack a closed breaker” rule is violated — potentially still carrying load or fault current; this is the direct justification for remote/motorised racking mechanisms in modern switchgear designs, letting the operator stand outside the arc flash boundary during the operation.
