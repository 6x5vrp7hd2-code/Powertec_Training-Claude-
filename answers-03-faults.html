Answers · Section 03

Faults — Overload, Short-Circuit, Earth Fault

Model answers and multiple-choice key.

Multiple Choice — Answer Key

1. What current range typically characterises an overload fault?
Correct answer: D — 1.1 to 3 times the rated current

2. What is “prospective short-circuit current” (Isc)?
Correct answer: D — The current that would flow at a given point if a solid short-circuit occurred there

3. Why is an earth fault described as “the most insidious” type of fault?
Correct answer: D — The resulting current depends entirely on the available return path, so it can be too small for ordinary protection to detect

4. What is “incident energy” as used in arc flash assessment?
Correct answer: C — The amount of thermal energy received at a given distance from an arc, measured in cal/cm²

5. Which protective device characteristic is designed to mirror the real heating behaviour of a cable during an overload?
Correct answer: A — A thermal or long-time release that tolerates the overload for seconds to minutes before tripping

Long Answer — Model Points

1. Explain why overload and short-circuit faults require fundamentally different types of protective response (timescale and mechanism), and how a modern circuit breaker’s trip unit provides both in one device.

Overload is a slow-developing thermal hazard (cable insulation ageing over minutes to hours) needing a delayed thermal-image response that tolerates brief excursions; short- circuit is an instantaneous, extremely high-magnitude event needing millisecond-scale tripping to prevent explosive arc energy release. A modern electronic trip unit combines both: the “L” (long time) function handles overload with a multi-second delay curve, while the “I” (instantaneous) and “S” (short time) functions handle short-circuit with no or minimal delay.

2. Describe the physical effects of an arc flash and explain why incident energy calculations matter for selecting PPE.

An arc flash releases enormous thermal energy (arc temperatures far exceeding the sun’s surface), creates a pressure wave, and can throw molten metal. Incident energy (cal/cm²) quantifies how much of that thermal energy would reach a worker at a defined distance, which directly determines the PPE category (arc-rated clothing, face shield, gloves) required to prevent second-degree burns.

3. Explain how the “return path” of an earth fault current depends on the earthing system in use, and what this means for whether an ordinary breaker or an RCD is needed.

If the return path is a low-impedance copper PE conductor (as in TN-S), fault current is large — comparable to a short-circuit — and an ordinary breaker sees and clears it quickly. If the return path is through soil (as in TT), impedance is high (10–100 Ω), fault current is only a few amps, and an ordinary overcurrent breaker cannot detect it; only an RCD, which compares outgoing and returning current rather than measuring an absolute threshold, can catch this fault.

4. What is a “short-circuit study” and why must it be performed before installing switchgear at any point in a data centre?

A short-circuit study calculates the prospective fault current (Isc) at every point in the installation based on source impedance and cable/transformer characteristics. It’s mandatory because the breaking capacity (Icu) of any switchgear installed must equal or exceed the Isc at that point — installing undersized switchgear risks it failing to interrupt a real fault, exploding instead of breaking.

5. Compare the danger profile (speed of onset, typical magnitude, typical consequence) of overload, short-circuit, and earth fault as described in the guide.

Overload: slow onset (minutes to hours), moderate magnitude (1.1–3× rated), consequence is progressive insulation damage/fire risk. Short-circuit: instantaneous onset (milliseconds), extremely high magnitude (tens of thousands of amps), consequence is arc flash, mechanical destruction. Earth fault: onset speed and magnitude depend entirely on the return path — can be as fast/large as a short-circuit (TN-S) or slow/small and undetectable to ordinary protection (TT) — consequence ranges from immediate tripping to a silently live, dangerous frame.
