Section 07

The Data Centre Power Chain — Overview

The guide’s redundancy topology table (N, N+1, 2N, distributed redundant) maps directly onto the industry-standard Uptime Institute Tier Classification System, the globally recognised benchmark for evaluating data centre infrastructure reliability, first created in 1993.

Additional context from current industry practice:

Uptime Institute Tiers and concurrent maintainability. Tier I facilities offer no redundancy — any maintenance means downtime — corresponding to the guide’s “N”

topology. Tier II adds redundant components (extra UPS modules, generators, chillers) but still only a single distribution path, so a distribution-path failure still causes an outage. Tier III facilities are concurrently maintainable: they have redundant components AND multiple independent distribution paths, so each and every component needed to support the IT load can be shut down and maintained without impacting operations — this directly corresponds to the guide’s N+1 and 2N/distributed-redundant topologies, and is the level the guide’s “reflex no. 1” (residual load calculation) exists to protect. Tier IV adds fault tolerance on top of concurrent maintainability: the facility can also absorb an unplanned single-component failure without any impact, typically through fully duplicated (2N or 2N+1) active paths.

Availability figures. Industry figures commonly associate Tier III with an availability target around 99.982% (roughly 1.6 hours of downtime per year) and Tier IV with around 99.995% (roughly 26 minutes per year) — illustrating that the jump from “concurrently maintainable” to “fault tolerant” represents a large jump in capital cost for a proportionally smaller (but operationally critical) uptime improvement.

Distributed redundancy (3N/2, catcher topologies). The guide’s “3N/2” example (chapter 12.5) reflects a real, widely used design pattern in hyperscale and colocation data centres: rather than a strict 2N (double everything), blocks of capacity are sized so that any one block can fail or be isolated while the remaining blocks absorb its load with a defined margin — offering most of 2N’s resilience at meaningfully lower capital cost, at the expense of a more complex, cross-linked load-transfer scheme that must be tracked precisely (exactly the caution the guide raises in section 12.5).
