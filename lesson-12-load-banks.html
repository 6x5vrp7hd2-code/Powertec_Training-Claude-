Lesson · Module 12

Load Banks

Read this in full before attempting the scenarios below — they assume this material.

A temporary connection is still a source-side connection

It's easy to think of a load bank as a piece of test equipment rather than as part of the electrical system, but for the duration of a test it is exactly that — a new, temporary connection on the source side of whatever is being tested. Isolation planning for the test period needs to include the load bank exactly as it would include any other source, not treat it as separate from the 'real' system.

Why load is applied in stages, not all at once

Generator and UPS load testing typically follows a staged profile — for example, a minimum two-hour test at 30% load for the first thirty minutes, 50% for the next thirty, then 100% for the final hour, consistent with common industry testing guidance such as NFPA 110. Applying load gradually allows problems to surface at lower risk before the equipment is pushed to full capacity, rather than discovering a fault only once 100% load is already connected.

Where the risk actually concentrates

Counter to the instinct that the test itself is the dangerous part, arc-flash risk during load bank testing is typically highest during the connection and disconnection of cables — not during the steady-state test period. PPE and procedural discipline matter most at exactly the moments that feel most routine.

Sequencing the connection and disconnection correctly

Correct breaker sequencing — typically connecting the load bank and closing its breaker first, then applying load from the source, with the building load path kept open throughout — exists to prevent an unintended parallel between the test and the live building load. The disconnection sequence at the end matters exactly as much: leaving the load bank breaker closed while restoring the building load path (or the reverse) can create the same unintended parallel, or an unintended open circuit, that the connection sequence was designed to avoid.
