Section 17

Interlocks — Read a Diagram and Never Be Caught Out by Them

Interlock philosophy — using physical or logical impossibility, rather than a rule someone might forget, to prevent a specific dangerous sequence — reflects a core principle of safety engineering sometimes called “inherently safe design” or “positive interlocking,” a concept found across many high-hazard industries (rail signalling, nuclear plant control, process safety), not just electrical switchgear.

Additional context:

Trapped-key systems as “physical proof.” The three named manufacturer systems (Castell, Ronis, Kirk) referenced by the guide represent the dominant commercial trapped-key interlock technologies used in industrial and utility switchgear worldwide. Their shared underlying principle — that possessing a physical key is itself unforgeable proof of an upstream device’s safe state — is deliberately more robust than any purely administrative or procedural control, because it doesn’t depend on anyone remembering to check a position or follow a step; the physical mechanism makes the wrong sequence literally impossible to execute, not just against the rules.

Interlock defeat as an exceptional, tightly controlled event. The guide’s rule that any interlock defeat requires written authorisation, a dedicated risk analysis, compensating measures, and a traced restoration reflects standard industrial safety practice for any temporary bypass of a safety interlock (closely analogous to “impairment” procedures used for fire protection system bypasses, or “management of change” processes in process safety) — the core idea being that removing a safety barrier, even briefly and for legitimate reasons, must be treated with at least as much rigor as the hazard the interlock itself protects against.

Zone Selective Interlocking (ZSI) as a logic-family interlock. ZSI, mentioned by the guide as a wired-logic alternative to pure time-based selectivity, allows a downstream breaker that detects a fault to signal upstream breakers to “wait” (i.e., not trip instantaneously), since the downstream device is already handling the fault — this reduces the overall fault-clearing time and arc flash energy at the upstream breaker compared to a purely time-graded selectivity scheme, where the upstream device must wait a fixed, conservative delay regardless of whether a downstream device actually responds.
